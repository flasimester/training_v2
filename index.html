Descent-Proof

An 8-day mountaineering and core cycle built around eccentric strength for the way down.

Foundation: slow-tempo eccentric & isometric work only.

Reactive: adds depth drops & hop-downs on Day 1, 3 & 6, now with lateral and unstable-surface variations. Stays at this dose as you progress — raise height, not frequency.

DAY 1
0/0

Lower Body — Eccentric Strength

Build the "brakes": muscles and tendons that control you on the way down.

Reminder: warm up ankles and hips first, and check your knees before loading — mild fatigue is fine, sharp or swollen means swap to mobility. Progress the step-down by height or load, not reps. Foam roll quads and calves afterward.

  • 3 min easy jog
  • Leg swings: front-to-back and side-to-side, 10 each leg
  • Walking lunges ×10
  • Bodyweight squats ×15
  • Glute bridges ×10
  • 2×5 bodyweight squats with a 2s pause at the bottom — groove the eccentric before loading it
  • Ankle circles and bodyweight calf raises ×10

DAY 4 — ACTIVE RECOVERY

Rest, or an Easy Spin

No lifting. This is where the tendons actually adapt — but a very easy gravel ride is a fine way to spend it.

Reminder: keep this day honest — if you're tempted to push the pace or add hills, take the full rest instead.

Pick one: a full rest day (walk, mobility, foam rolling), or 30–45 minutes of gravel riding at a genuinely easy, conversational pace (zone 1–2, low heart rate). Keep it flat-ish and easy on the legs — this day is about blood flow, not training stress.

Why: this is where Days 1–3 actually turn into strength. An easy spin can help circulation, but only if it stays easy — push the pace and you're borrowing from Day 5 instead of resting.

DAY 7

Long Endurance

Build the endurance base with a long ride; let Day 1, 3 and 5 carry the downhill-specific work.

Reminder: pack water and electrolytes — this is the longest session in the cycle.

  • 5 min easy spin or walk, building pace gradually
  • Dynamic leg swings, 10 each leg
  • A few gentle accelerations (on the bike) or a faster few minutes uphill (on foot) to open the legs before settling into zone 2

A 90–150 min zone-2 gravel or road ride, steady effort, nose-breathing pace. Progress by adding 15–20 min of ride time every 1–2 cycles. Finish with an easy core circuit: plank 3×45s, dead bug 2×10/side, side plank 2×30s/side.

Prefer to be on foot, or no bike that day? Swap in a loaded pack hike instead, ideally with elevation gain — even a stairwell or parking garage ramp beats flat ground for the downhill-specific benefit. Either way, the step-downs, Spanish squat and tendon isometrics on Day 1, 3 and 5 are doing the actual downhill-specific work.

Phase 2 add-on: no descent available on the ride, so add 3–4 sets of 10 slow backward walking lunges with a loaded pack afterward — it loads the quads eccentrically in a way riding doesn't.
Why: strength only becomes useful once it meets real time under load. Flat terrain won't replicate a descent, but it still builds the aerobic engine climbing and long days depend on.
DAY 8 — RECOVERY

Rest Before the Cycle Restarts

Full rest, or an easy walk and mobility. Day 7 is the biggest aerobic and mechanical load in the cycle — this day is what lets you absorb it.

Reminder: keep this day honest too — no sneaking in hard effort. Let Day 7 actually be absorbed before Day 1 restarts.

No lifting, no hard riding. Light stretching, foam rolling, or a short easy walk are all fine. Day 1 restarts fresh tomorrow.

Why: a long effort and its recovery belong together. Skip this one and the fatigue carries straight into next cycle's Day 1.

How to run this

  • The cycle is 8 days long (3 on, 1 off, 3 on, 1 off) and simply repeats — it doesn't need to line up with the calendar week.
  • Progress load every 1–2 weeks: add weight, add a rep, or add hold time, not all three at once. For step-downs and lunges specifically, progress box height or added load first — reps are a distant second priority.
  • Eccentric quad strength is the single biggest priority in this program for descents — if you're short on time, protect Day 1's step-downs and Spanish squat before anything else.
  • Both calf raise variants matter: straight-knee (Day 1) hits the gastrocnemius, bent-knee/seated (Day 5) hits the soleus. Uneven terrain uses both.
  • The program deliberately keeps only one heavy hip-hinge per cycle (the Day 5 deadlift) — the single-leg RDL on Day 1 is light and unilateral, so it doesn't compete with it.
  • If a long gravel ride is coming up, put your heaviest leg day (Day 1 or Day 5) at least one full day before it, not the day before.
  • Knee pain rule: mild muscle fatigue is fine; sharp, localized, or swelling-associated knee pain means stop that exercise and, if it persists, see a physio or sports doctor — this program supports healthy joints, it doesn't replace a diagnosis.
  • Everything here also crosses over to gravel biking: the carries and grip work protect your hands and forearms on rough gravel, and the core/hip work stabilizes your pedal stroke out of the saddle.
  • Sleep and protein are part of the program — tendons adapt slowly and mostly during recovery, not during the set.
  • Deload every 4–6 weeks: keep the same exercises but drop volume by roughly 40% (fewer sets, lighter isometric holds) for one full cycle. This is when tendons actually catch up to the muscle strength you've built.
  • Tendons adapt on a slower timeline than muscle — expect visible strength gains in 2–3 weeks but real tendon resilience to take 8–12 weeks of consistent, un-skipped isometric and eccentric work.
  • Log how your knees feel after Day 3 and Day 7 specifically — those are the two sessions that most resemble a real descent, so they're your earliest warning sign if something's off.
  • Uphill performance is mostly an endurance problem. Keep Day 7 and the optional zone-2 ride consistent, since they build the aerobic base that strength work alone can't.
  • Rice bucket and grip work is low-stakes to skip if you're short on time; the leg and core days are the ones that matter most for the downhill goal.
